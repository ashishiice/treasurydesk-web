**The 28-year precedent — US buying another country's currency.** Something just happened that hasn't occurred in 28 years: the US government is spending its money to save another country's currency. Japan and the US warned of further coordinated action in FX markets, confirming the first joint intervention in 15 years to lift the yen from a near four-decade low. Trump framed it as a signal of friendship with Tokyo — "we're very strong, very, very strong financially and they have a weakening yen and they wanted a little bit of help." He added the aside "with the exception, of course, of Pearl Harbor." The key reveal was how the world found out: a Reuters photographer captured a note belonging to Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent reading "To do: buy Japanese yen 5 to 10 billion." That photo was published for everyone to see, confirming the US Treasury is intervening in the Japanese currency market.

**Was the note accidental or strategic?** Two theories are presented. Theory one: it's a genuine to-do list and the US is genuinely buying yen. Theory two: the photo was leaked on purpose to trick investors, because someone as sophisticated as Bessent would not need to write down a to-do list to buy billions of a currency, nor would he need to write the ticker "JPY" as if he didn't know it, nor be that specific about "5 to 10 billion." The specificity is suspicious — it reads as theatre, a signal engineered to move positioning without the Treasury having to formally announce a weak-dollar policy.

**The bond market's verdict — the dollar must be sacrificed.** The core thesis is a "trilemma": the US cannot simultaneously sustain (1) high spending and rising debt, (2) high interest rates, and (3) a strong dollar. Every point higher on the yield makes the $40 trillion of debt more expensive to carry, which means even more borrowing, which means more bonds to issue — a vicious loop. That's why the 30-year bond yield keeps going up. The bond market appears to have concluded that the only variable that can be sacrificed without an immediate crisis is the dollar — but it must be weakened subtly and deniably, because an explicit "we are devaluing" announcement would trigger a treasury sell-off by foreign holders, crashing the dollar and forcing rates up to counterbalance. The analogy used is paying down a credit card with a credit card.

**The mechanics — sell euros, not dollars, to buy yen.** When the US bought the yen it did not sell dollars to do it. It sold euros — the only foreign money the US holds. As a result the yen went up, the dollar went down, but on paper the US never sold a single dollar. Because the dollar is always measured relative to something, the "something" they are choosing as the reference is the yen. Sacrificing the dollar via this route causes a slow crisis rather than an immediate one — less noticeable, plausibly deniable.

**Japan as America's banker — the structural reason this matters.** Japan is the biggest foreign holder of US government debt, over a trillion dollars of treasuries — more than any other country — accumulated over 40 years of selling cars, chips, machines and Pokémon cards and lending the dollars back by buying US bonds. This funded US deficits because Japan had no better option: their rates were zero, US rates were not. Everything changed when the yen started to collapse. When the yen strengthens fast, the yen carry trade unwinds — everybody sells US assets to pay back yen, and markets worldwide correct or crash (the August 2024 global selloff was a small preview). The longer the yen weakens, the closer Japan gets to being forced to strengthen it, either by raising rates (slowly underway) or by selling US treasuries to raise dollars to buy back its own currency (also happening). Both roads lead to America's biggest creditor selling US debt exactly when the US needs to borrow more — worsened by Middle East conflict spending.

**Why the US is "rescuing" Japan — it's self-preservation.** The US is not rescuing Japan out of generosity. If Japan goes down it takes the US bond market with it, along with global markets. A weaker dollar helps America reshore; a stronger yen keeps Japan's pension money from panic-selling US treasuries. US and Japanese interests align: both need the dollar down and the yen up. That's why Bessent is buying the yen — or at least showing the illusion of it.

**The endgame — everything goes down first, then the printer turns on.** Citing Luke Groman: if that's the plan, they're on track. The sequence is everything goes down first — including gold and Bitcoin, as seen in the past 6 weeks — and when the pain gets bad enough (long-term Treasury rates spiking, triggered by Middle East conflict or another shock), the printer turns on, and the things they can't print more of start to go up. Timing is unknowable — could be this year, next year, or five years out. There will never be a press conference announcing dollar weakening or money printing; the whole point is deniability. The leaked note and the euro-for-yen swaps are breadcrumbs in that deniability.

**What to watch — the signal indicators.** He is tracking the 10- and 30-year Treasury bonds, the strength of the yen, and the price of oil and gold. If the 30-year yield keeps rising while stocks fall, pressure is building and we're getting closer to the printer. If gold rises while the dollar falls, the debasement trade is back.
